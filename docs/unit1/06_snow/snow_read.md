# Pre-Class - Snow

This lesson covers snow hydrology, which dominates the water supply in Utah and the Mountain West: how the lapse rate decides whether precipitation falls as rain or snow, snow water equivalent (SWE), the energy balance that drives snowmelt and the role of albedo, and the degree-day method for estimating melt from air temperature. It also points to historical and real-time snow and water data sources.

## Reading

## Pre-Class Questions

### Question 1 - Why Snow Matters in Hydrology

In one or two sentences, explain why snow is so hydrologically important in the Mountain West states like Utah, even though only about 13% of national precipitation falls as snow.

### Question 2 - Snow vs. Rain and the Lapse Rate

In one or two sentences, explain how a lapse rate determines whether precipitation falls as rain or snow at a given elevation, and use a quick example.

### Question 3 - Snow Water Equivalent (SWE)

Define snow water equivalent (SWE) and explain why two snowpacks of equal depth can carry very different amounts of water.

### Question 4 - Energy Sources for Snowmelt and Albedo

Snowmelt is driven by an energy balance at the snowpack surface. In one or two sentences, name the dominant energy source on a typical clear melt day and explain why fresh snow tends to delay the start of melt while aged snow accelerates it.

### Question 5 - The Degree-Day Melt Method

State the degree-day equation for snowmelt, identify each variable, and give one reason hydrologists still use this simple temperature-index method even when full energy-balance models exist.
